The problem, and what we do

The hardest part of water is the walk.

In the villages we work with, the nearest safe source is often an hour or more away. That walk falls mostly on girls and women, every day, and it costs school, work and rest. A tap close to home gives those hours back.

  1. The village asks, and leads

    We only work where a community invites us. Families choose the site, give labour and elect a water committee — half of its seats held by women.

  2. Local crews build it

    Tanzanian and Ugandan drilling teams build boreholes, solar pumps, rain tanks and tap stands, sized for the village and tested for quality.

  3. We stay for ten years

    Committees learn repairs and bookkeeping. Our mechanics visit every water point each quarter, and a sensor tells us if a pump stops.

Stories from the villages

What changes when the water comes close.

“I used to leave before light and come back after the children had gone to school. Now I see them off, and I'm back from the tap before the tea is cool.”

Neema A.Treasurer, Mlimani water committee

Neema keeps the committee's books: every family pays the equivalent of 40 cents a month into a repair fund. When the pump's controller failed last March, the committee paid for the part themselves and had water back in two days.

School tap · Bukedi

Taps before lessons

A rain tank and handwashing station at Bukedi Primary. Absences from stomach illness fell by more than half in the first term.

Tap stand · Kiwangala

Small hands, short trip

Six tap stands now sit within 400 m of every home in Kiwangala. Fetching water has become a five-minute errand.
